## Translating one school form takes two weeks of notice.

It started with one principal's complaint. The team traced it to a workflow gap: messages translate automatically, and attachments wait on the district translation unit. A family waiting on a special education plan or a health form gets it late, or reads it through their child:

## Interviews, an audit and a policy review landed on attachments.

### 12 stakeholder interviews

**Why:** District approval for research involving student data would outlast a four week sprint, so the team used guerrilla recruiting to reach administrators, counselors and teachers across Bay Area schools.

### Platform audit

**Why:** The team audited the district's family messaging platform from two accounts, then mapped competitors and adjacent school tools to see where document translation falls through.

### Policy and legal review

**Why:** State education code, district policy and federal privacy law showed who holds the translation duty and what unreviewed machine translation puts at risk.

The product lead ran outreach across 63 contacts. I led the interviews and synthesis. The design systems lead built the design system, and the user flows lead mapped the user flows.

I synthesized the 12 interviews into 15 affinity clusters in FigJam.

## Two findings changed who the product serves and how it works.

| | The original idea: ParentSquare translator | What research led to: District platform |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | The messaging platform | School districts <sup>1</sup> |
| Who translates | Third party translation model | AI, checked by an administrator <sup>1</sup> |
| Accuracy | Machine output, no human check | Human approval before delivery <sup>2</sup> |
| Privacy | Records leave district control | Records stay under district control <sup>2</sup> |

### Why it changed: finding 1

State law puts the translation duty on the district, and only the district's board can sign the contract.

**What the team did:** After an engineer and a design consultant pointed the team toward schools, the product lead made the district the buyer. The UX and UI leads rebuilt the flows and screens around school staff.

**Changes:** Buyer · Who translates

<Sources>Cal. Educ. Code § 48985 · OUSD Board Policy 5124 · Cal. Educ. Code § 17604</Sources>

### Why it changed: finding 2

Unreviewed machine translation of student records creates Title VI and FERPA exposure.

**What the team did:** Districts already have a person check vital translations, so the team made that step part of the product. I introduced the review flow, and the UX and UI leads rebuilt the user flow and screens around it, including a verification screen with the source and translation side by side.

**Changes:** Accuracy · Privacy

<Sources>U.S. v. Cherry Creek School District settlement, 2024 · 34 CFR § 99.31(a)(1)(i)(B) · Orange County Dept. of Education RFP 26-01, 2026</Sources>

Competing school apps tell schools their document translation "is not intended for use with legal documents." AthenaScribe was built for those documents. It flags them on upload and routes them to a person.

## The new design held up in testing, and the queue needed sorting.

The two findings above put a person in charge of approving vital documents. We gave that step to the administrators who would run it and asked two things: can they use it, and does it fit their day?

### As tested

1. Scan
2. Translate
3. Right person

### Can a school administrator complete the approval flow?

<p class="label">Research question · Phone</p>

**Yes, with guidance.** All 3 tasks completed. Ease of navigation rated 8.5 out of 10.

### Does the review step fit an administrator's workload?

<p class="label">Research question · Laptop</p>

**Not at high school volume.** With 120 teachers sending forms, reviewing every one would not fit an administrator's day.

<Quote source="Administrator, round one usability testing">"I would like buckets to where it says, okay, health forms, field trip forms. Wellness checks."</Quote>

### Next version

- **Severity 3:** Documents sorted into buckets by type, with flags for the types an administrator wants to see.
- **Severity 2:** The refresh control shows a visible update with a timestamp.

Round one: live prototype · Informed by administrators, counselors, community school staff and nonprofit leaders across the Bay Area · Tasks: upload, work the review queue, approve for delivery · Measures: task completion, think aloud, rating questions

## Translation is automatic. Approval stays human.

Staff upload a document. AI translates it and flags the vital ones. A person approves before it sends.

1. Staff upload
2. Language from school records
3. Sorted by document type
4. Sent to the right reviewer
5. Family reads and signs

### Refinements from testing

- **Language:** Reads each family's home language from Aeries, so no one chooses it again.
- **Text size:** Larger type and a wider layout that hold at 200 percent zoom. Severity 3.
- **Read aloud:** Now a labeled button, since its purpose was unclear. Severity 2.
- **Opened by:** Each document shows "Opened by X of Y families" and a trend. Red stays reserved for problems.

## A safeguard nobody has time to use is still a delay.

I designed the review step for accuracy first. Testing showed speed matters just as much, so the next version puts a deadline on every vital document, visible to staff and administrators.
